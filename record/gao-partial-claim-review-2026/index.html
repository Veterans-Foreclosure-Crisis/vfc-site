VA Replaced Its Canceled Foreclosure Relief Program. GAO Says It Still Can’t Prove the New One Works.

Veterans have spent years caught between expired mortgage assistance, delayed replacements, unaffordable loan modifications, and foreclosures. A new federal watchdog report raises serious questions about VA’s latest program—and what happens to families already harmed.

Veterans Foreclosure Crisis | Research & Accountability Review | November 14, 2026 — Owner-approved editorial draft; NOT published

On November 10, 2026, the Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress’s independent investigative agency, released its first report examining the Department of Veterans Affairs’ new Partial Claim Program.

The 37-page report, GAO-27-108908, confirms that VA has taken steps to implement the program. It also finds that VA still lacks a comprehensive plan to determine whether the program works.

That finding matters. But understanding its significance requires looking at what happened before the new program existed.

Veterans have spent years navigating temporary COVID-era assistance, changing mortgage rules, delayed program launches, unaffordable loan modifications, and foreclosure proceedings.

VA and Congress have repeatedly tried to correct problems created or left unresolved by earlier programs. Each change has required mortgage servicers to adjust their systems and procedures while borrowers continued facing deadlines.

A program being announced does not mean veterans can use it. A mortgage being modified does not mean the payment is affordable. And a foreclosure being avoided today does not tell us whether a family will still own its home next year.

GAO’s latest findings raise questions about all three.

This review examines what GAO found, how those findings connect to the broader foreclosure crisis, what the government has already been warned about, and what VA and Congress still need to answer.

How did we get here? Years of temporary fixes and repeated gaps

During the COVID-19 pandemic, federal mortgage forbearance allowed many homeowners to temporarily pause payments during financial hardship.

But forbearance did not erase the missed payments.

Veterans eventually needed a way to catch up.

For borrowers who could afford their original monthly mortgage payments, one solution was a partial claim: move missed payments into a separate, interest-free balance that could be repaid later, rather than replacing the entire mortgage with a higher-interest loan.

VA introduced its temporary COVID-19 Partial Claim Program in July 2021. It expired on October 28, 2022.

Many veterans were still recovering from COVID-related hardships when the program ended.

VA continued offering other mortgage-assistance options, including loan modifications. But rising interest rates made some modifications substantially more expensive.

Veterans who had purchased or refinanced homes at historically low interest rates could face significantly higher monthly payments if their mortgages were modified at current market rates.

Housing advocates warned about this problem.

In February 2024, National Consumer Law Center attorney Steve Sharpe told Congress that VA had allowed its partial-claim option to expire while veterans remained in forbearance.

The practical problem was straightforward: some veterans could afford their original mortgage payments but could not afford the new payments required by the available modification options.

Source: NCLC testimony, February 15, 2024.

Then came VASP.

VA created the Veterans Affairs Servicing Purchase Program (VASP) to help eligible borrowers who could not obtain affordable assistance through other available options.

VASP allowed VA to purchase certain distressed mortgages and provide modified terms intended to make the loans more manageable.

The program began accepting submissions on May 31, 2024.

But that was not the same as full implementation.

VA gave mortgage servicers until October 1, 2024, to implement the program.

On October 1, VA acknowledged that some servicers still had not finished.

On December 12, 2024, VA officials reported that approximately 90% of VA-guaranteed loans were held by servicers participating in VASP.

Even then, VA continued working with servicers that had not fully implemented the program.

VASP existed for approximately eleven months, but its original full-implementation deadline came four months after launch.

From October 1, 2024, to its cancellation on May 1, 2025, there were only seven months.

From VA’s December implementation update to cancellation, fewer than five months remained.

Neither date establishes that every eligible veteran could actually obtain VASP assistance. Those were implementation milestones, not verified universal-access dates.

And the rollout was not occurring under a universal foreclosure prohibition.

VA had strongly encouraged servicers to implement a targeted foreclosure moratorium through December 31, 2024, with specified exceptions. That guidance did not guarantee every borrower was protected.

Sources: VA’s May 29, 2024 VASP circular; October 1 implementation update; December 12 VA press conference.

Then VA canceled VASP.

On April 23, 2025, VA announced that it would stop accepting new VASP submissions beginning May 1.

VA allowed already accepted trial payment plans to continue through August 31, 2025, with purchases subject to available funds. The May 1 cutoff applied to new submissions.

The agency cited concerns about its role in purchasing and restructuring distressed mortgages.

Ending VASP did not eliminate the underlying mortgage defaults.

It did not restore borrowers’ previous interest rates.

And it did not provide an immediately operational replacement.

Congress enacted the VA Home Loan Program Reform Act on July 30, 2025, authorizing a new Partial Claim Program.

That program did not become effective until June 15, 2026—more than thirteen months after VASP stopped accepting new submissions.

And the replacement is being phased in.

Mortgage servicers have until November 28, 2026, to complete implementation.

The new program also has a statutory sunset. It is authorized through July 30, 2030, rather than being a permanent assistance program.

Sources: VA’s VASP termination circular; GAO-27-108908.

Mortgage servicers were warning about the same problem

The repeated changes did not affect veterans alone. Mortgage servicers had to adjust their systems, train employees, update eligibility procedures, and implement changing VA requirements. Borrowers needed clear, written answers about what would happen while a new program was not yet operational.

The September 16, 2026, House Veterans’ Affairs Committee Minority roundtable raised these implementation and guidance concerns. But VFC’s existing public evidence record explicitly places several attributed statements from the committee-caption account on HOLD pending comparison with original audio and underlying written records.

That includes a reported aggregate count of servicers that had implemented the new program, a numerical breakdown of industry questions and written VA answers, and an account of interim direction involving market-rate loan modifications. Those figures and speaker attributions are not established findings in this article. They must not be used as a measure of verified servicer readiness or as proof of an agency-wide written policy.

The underlying questions remain consequential: Which servicers were operational on which dates? What written guidance governed borrowers whose servicers had not implemented the new waterfall? What happened to applications and foreclosure proceedings during that interval? What original documents and audio corroborate the reported exchanges?

VA’s own servicer FAQ confirms that servicers must implement the Loss Mitigation Waterfall and Partial Claim together and were given an implementation period. It does not, by itself, verify the roundtable’s disputed numerical or attribution claims.

Source status: HOLD for original-audio and document reconciliation. VFC’s September 16 roundtable record provides the timestamped questions, limitations, and links to the independently sourced servicer tracker. Original records are needed before asserting specific figures or attributing interim directions to VA.

Congress had already been warned about foreclosures during the gap

The danger was not hypothetical.

During consideration of H.R. 1815 in May 2025, lawmakers proposed adding temporary foreclosure protections while VA developed replacement assistance.

Rep. Mark Takano offered a 90-day foreclosure-moratorium amendment. It failed in the House Veterans’ Affairs Committee by an 11–13 vote.

The final legislation authorized a new Partial Claim Program without adopting that proposed moratorium.

On May 26, 2026, Rep. Chris Pappas and 27 other House members sent VA Secretary Doug Collins a letter requesting an immediate, targeted foreclosure moratorium.

They warned that veterans were still at risk while the new program was being finalized and implemented.

The letter requested a written response by June 2, including VA’s legal basis if the agency declined to implement the pause.

On June 15, the day the new Partial Claim Program launched, NCLC renewed its warning.

Steve Sharpe urged VA to direct servicers not to proceed with foreclosures until their Partial Claim processes were operational and not to offer payment-increasing modifications to borrowers who would qualify for the new program.

These were requests for additional protections, not evidence that VA had adopted them.

The distinction matters.

Congress authorized the replacement program, but the legislation did not automatically protect every eligible veteran from foreclosure while servicers implemented it.

Sources: Pappas’s May 26 letter; NCLC, June 15, 2026; VFC legislative record.

What the latest GAO report actually found

GAO’s November 10 report examines the development and implementation of VA’s new Partial Claim Program.

It confirms that VA developed policies, sought stakeholder input, trained servicers, modified data systems, and established implementation procedures.

Those are meaningful steps.

But GAO found that VA still lacks a comprehensive evaluation plan.

VA identified measures it intends to track, including approvals, denials, trial-payment completion, future defaults, and repayment outcomes.

GAO’s concern is that collecting those measures alone will not establish whether the program is effective or whether changes in outcomes are actually attributable to the program.

GAO recommended that VA promptly develop a comprehensive evaluation plan.

The recommendation remains open.

VA provided no comments on the report.

What GAO did not investigate

The report provides useful background on earlier COVID-era assistance problems.

But it is not a comprehensive investigation of the entire 2020–2026 veteran foreclosure crisis.

It does not independently reconstruct the full consequences of VASP’s cancellation, determine whether every servicer followed the applicable requirements, or establish what remedies should be available to families already displaced.

Those questions fall outside much of the report’s assigned scope.

They should not be mistaken for resolved issues.

GAO’s finding is important precisely because the government is implementing another replacement program without a comprehensive plan to measure its results.

Source: GAO-27-108908.

November 28 is an implementation deadline, not proof of successful assistance

The new Partial Claim Program became effective June 15, 2026.

Servicers have until November 28 to implement it.

GAO reported that additional VA data-system functionality was expected in January 2027, and VA officials anticipated beginning program trend and outcome analysis in December 2027.

That leaves three separate questions.

Was the program deployed?

Can VA and mortgage servicers process the required applications, eligibility decisions, trial plans, claims, payments, and corrections?

Could eligible veterans actually use it?

Were borrowers evaluated correctly? Were decisions timely? Were claims completed before foreclosure deadlines? What happened when a servicer was not ready?

Did it work for families?

Were payments affordable? Did borrowers remain current? How many fell behind again? How many ultimately lost their homes?

VA’s public implementation announcements do not answer all three.

The November 28 deadline provides a concrete opportunity to test operational readiness.

It does not establish that every eligible borrower received assistance.

VA’s success numbers need more context

VA has reported substantial numbers of veterans helped through mortgage assistance.

Its FY2025 Annual Benefits Report says 173,478 borrowers were helped to retain homeownership and/or avoid foreclosure.

But that administrative figure does not independently establish how many borrowers obtained affordable, lasting relief.

GAO identifies limitations in VA’s existing default-resolution measures, including that loans can become current without a specific loss-mitigation intervention.

That distinction matters when VA attributes outcomes to its assistance programs.

In October 2026, NPR reported that 63% of borrowers in an examined VA mortgage-modification dataset received modifications that increased their monthly payments.

More than 15,000 borrowers experienced increases of at least $150, including more than 700 whose payments increased by $1,000 or more.

Those figures describe NPR’s examined population, not every VA mortgage.

But they raise a serious question about what VA counts as successful assistance.

A borrower who accepts a higher payment because foreclosure is the alternative should not automatically be treated as proof that affordable assistance succeeded.

Sources: VA FY2025 Loan Guaranty Report; NPR, October 30, 2026.

GAO’s foreclosure data deserves closer examination

GAO’s appendix provides quarterly data on active VA-guaranteed loans, delinquencies, mortgage assistance, and completed foreclosures.

Among its reported figures:

The data establishes that VA maintains substantial portfolio-level reporting.

What remains missing from the public record is a reliable connection between individual assistance methods, payment affordability, repeat defaults, foreclosure outcomes, and the timing of program availability.

GAO also notes that VA cannot produce certain modification summaries broken down by modification type.

That limits independent evaluation of which interventions borrowers received and how they performed.

Source: GAO report, Appendix I.

Previous administrative failures also need answers

GAO reports that VA officials identified extensive documentation problems under the earlier COVID-era Partial Claim Program.

According to those officials, approximately 70% of submitted collateral documents contained errors.

VA also reported approximately $139 million in contracting costs associated with that earlier program.

The problems described included incorrect loan balances, inaccurate loan identifiers, and documents addressed to the wrong agency.

Those are significant administrative concerns.

But GAO’s report does not independently establish which contractors caused the errors, how many borrowers were harmed, or whether particular foreclosures resulted from those failures.

The original contracts, quality-control findings, transaction records, and corrective-action documentation remain necessary.

VA redesigned the new program to place greater responsibility on mortgage servicers and automate certain processes.

That may address some earlier problems.

But moving responsibility between VA and servicers does not establish that the new controls work.

Who verifies the calculations? Who corrects errors? And what happens to the borrower while the agencies and servicers sort them out?

Those are implementation questions, not merely administrative details.

Fiscal responsibility requires examining the consequences of policy decisions

VA Secretary Doug Collins ended VASP in May 2025. House Veterans’ Affairs Committee Chairman Mike Bost and Rep. Derrick Van Orden supported its termination.

The decision was defended in part through concerns about VASP’s cost and VA’s role in purchasing distressed mortgages.

Fiscal accountability matters.

The VA Home Loan Guaranty Program involves federal guarantees, borrower funding fees, statutory exemptions, credit-subsidy accounting, servicing expenses, foreclosure claims, and recoveries.

Those categories must be evaluated accurately.

But the cost of operating an assistance program cannot be examined separately from the cost of terminating it.

What happened to borrowers who might otherwise have qualified for VASP?

How many accepted unaffordable modifications?

How many subsequently defaulted?

How much did completed foreclosures cost the federal guarantee program?

What did it cost to implement another replacement?

And what happened to families who lost homes during the transition?

These are questions for original financial and borrower-level records, not assumptions.

The decision to terminate VASP was not the beginning of the crisis. Earlier COVID-era program changes and servicing problems also require examination.

But the May 2025 decision, the subsequent thirteen-month replacement gap, and the absence of a comprehensive evaluation plan are documented parts of the accountability record.

If fiscal responsibility is the justification for changing a program, the government should measure the actual financial consequences of that decision—including what happened when assistance was unavailable.

Read: VFC Policy & Fiscal Analysis.

The families GAO’s program evaluation could miss

The new Partial Claim Program may help eligible borrowers avoid future foreclosures.

But it does not automatically resolve the situations of veterans affected by previous program gaps.

That includes borrowers who accepted higher-payment modifications, borrowers denied assistance under earlier rules, families whose homes were sold in foreclosure, and veterans who have already been displaced.

A program evaluation limited to new Partial Claim recipients would not fully account for those households.

The April 2026 NPR investigation documented the consequences of VASP’s cancellation, including the experience of a disabled Marine veteran family in Spokane, Washington.

That family’s account illustrates why the original servicing history matters.

An agency’s statement that it provided assistance does not, by itself, establish that the borrower received an affordable resolution or that every applicable option was correctly evaluated.

Individual disputes require original records and careful review.

But the larger problem extends beyond one household.

VFC’s research identifies several groups of veterans who need different answers.

Who is still waiting for answers—and what relief is available?

1. Veterans affected by the expiration of COVID-era Partial Claims

What happened to borrowers who were still recovering from COVID-related hardships after the temporary program expired in October 2022?

Were they offered affordable alternatives?

How many received higher-rate modifications or entered foreclosure?

Needed: A review of the affected loans, applicable policies, assistance decisions, and subsequent outcomes.

2. Veterans affected by VASP’s phased rollout

What happened to borrowers whose mortgage servicers were not ready to process VASP applications between May and late 2024?

Were foreclosure protections followed?

Were eligible loans held for later evaluation, as VA instructed?

Needed: Servicer implementation records, dated eligibility reviews, foreclosure actions, and documentation of compliance with the applicable guidance.

3. Veterans affected by VASP’s cancellation

What happened to borrowers who had not completed VASP processing when VA stopped accepting new submissions on May 1, 2025?

How many were subsequently offered higher-payment modifications?

How many lost their homes before replacement assistance became available?

Needed: A reconciled cohort analysis, original servicing records, and review of any legally available corrective measures.

4. Veterans caught in the new Partial Claim rollout

Which borrowers have been unable to access the new program because their servicers were not ready?

Were they offered payment-increasing modifications instead?

Did foreclosures proceed while assistance was unavailable?

Needed: Per-servicer implementation evidence, application and denial records, payment outcomes, and documented foreclosure protections.

5. Veterans who received assistance but remain at risk

How many borrowers are current only because they accepted substantially higher payments?

How many have fallen behind again?

What assistance exists when the original modification was unaffordable or the borrower experiences another hardship?

Needed: Before-and-after payment data, repeat-default rates, and evaluation of legally available affordability measures.

6. Veterans whose homes have already been foreclosed upon

What happened to the families who lost their homes before the replacement program became available?

Were the original servicing and foreclosure decisions correct under the rules that applied at the time?

What remedies, reviews, or other assistance are legally available now?

Needed: Consent-protected case review, original agency and servicer records, identification of errors where supported, and evaluation of possible remedies.

7. Families facing continuing displacement or post-sale consequences

What happened after the foreclosure?

Did families face eviction, additional debt, damaged credit, housing instability, or barriers to obtaining another home?

Which federal, state, or local programs can assist them?

Needed: A separate examination of post-foreclosure consequences and available legal, financial, and housing remedies.

These groups are not mutually exclusive. A family may have experienced several successive program gaps.

They also do not all qualify for the same assistance or remedy.

The government cannot measure the full impact of its foreclosure policies by counting only the borrowers who successfully entered the newest program.

What needs to happen next?

VA must demonstrate that the November 28 implementation deadline has produced operational access to the new Partial Claim Program.

Congress must examine GAO’s open evaluation recommendation and require meaningful measures of borrower affordability, repeat defaults, and sustained home retention.

The House and Senate Veterans’ Affairs Committees should examine the consequences of the earlier program transitions, including the decisions and warnings that preceded them.

Original VA, contractor, and mortgage-servicer records should be preserved and reviewed to determine what went wrong, what was corrected, and what remains unresolved.

And families already harmed need a separate response.

Some corrective actions may fall within existing VA authority. Others may require legislation, legal proceedings, or individualized review.

Those distinctions matter. So does protecting private borrower information and obtaining consent before using family case records.

But none of those limitations justifies treating the families who already lost their homes as outside the problem.

What can you do?

If you are a veteran or family member facing foreclosure: Contact your servicer immediately, request a written evaluation of available VA assistance, and preserve your documents. If a sale or eviction deadline is approaching, seek qualified legal or housing-counseling assistance. Do not assume a pending application or complaint automatically stops foreclosure.

Start with VFC’s First 72 Hours guide.

If you have already lost your home or accepted an unaffordable modification: Preserve your payment histories, applications, correspondence, foreclosure notices, and servicing decisions. These records may help establish what happened and whether any review or remedy is available.

If you work in housing counseling, mortgage servicing, legal aid, or veterans’ services: Help identify recurring implementation problems, missing written guidance, and gaps between announced policies and actual borrower outcomes. Share public evidence and protect confidential client information.

If you are a journalist or researcher: Examine the difference between reported foreclosure avoidance, actual affordability, sustained home retention, and completed foreclosure. Preserve dates, populations, definitions, and source limitations.

If you are a congressional office or policymaker: Request the original implementation records, GAO evaluation plan, foreclosure and modification outcomes, and evidence of what happened to the earlier affected cohorts. Determine what protections and remedies are available under existing law and what additional authority may be necessary.

Visit VFC’s Take Action page for the existing source-linked accountability record and ways to participate.

The question Congress still needs to answer

GAO has now documented that VA’s latest foreclosure-relief program is being implemented without a comprehensive plan to determine whether it works.

But the families affected by the earlier programs are still waiting for answers.

Some were caught when COVID-era assistance expired.

Some waited while VASP was being implemented.

Some were excluded when VASP ended.

Some accepted higher mortgage payments because they saw no workable alternative.

Some are waiting for the new Partial Claim Program.

And some have already lost their homes.

What happened to each of these groups? Which government and servicer decisions affected them? What protections were required? What remedies remain available?

Those questions deserve documented answers.

VA and Congress have spent years developing temporary solutions while balancing program costs, administrative requirements, and federal financial responsibilities.

Veterans have spent those same years trying to keep their homes.

Follow the law. Verify the assistance. Stop preventable foreclosures and evictions. Investigate the losses that have already occurred.

Veteran families should not have to keep paying for the government’s next temporary fix.

November 14 evidence update: eligibility is not the same as implementation

VA’s servicer FAQ, last updated October 23, 2026, explains that a paid COVID-era Partial Claim or Refund Modification on the same VA-guaranteed loan disqualifies the borrower from another Partial Claim. The VA Servicer Handbook, Chapter 22, § 22.02(a)(7) also excludes borrowers who received a loan modification in the 24 months before the servicer evaluates the loan under the loss-mitigation waterfall. GAO discusses both restrictions on page 6, note 13. VA applies separate timing rules when evaluating eligibility for another loan modification.

VA also says servicers must implement the Loss Mitigation Waterfall and Partial Claim together, and that a foreclosure initiated by the mortgage servicer does not by itself trigger the FAQ’s exclusion for foreclosures initiated by another entity, such as an HOA.

These distinctions make borrower-level records and the actual applicable VA waterfall essential. They also raise an unresolved cohort question: how many veterans received earlier assistance that now excludes them from the new program, or were steered into a higher-payment modification while another option was unavailable? The published FAQ does not answer that population-level question or establish that any individual borrower was evaluated correctly. The restrictions and available remedies should be assessed against the operative M26-4 guidance and the facts of each loan.
